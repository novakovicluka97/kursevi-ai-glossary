MCP, from zero
01 / 05
01
// the locked-out assistant

Imagine you hire a brilliant assistant.

First day on the job. Sharp, capable, ready to work. But there is a problem: they have no logins, no badges, no keys to anything in your office.

🧑‍💼
Claude
your new assistant
🚫 locked out
📧 📅 📁
your apps
Gmail, Calendar, Drive

You ask them to "pull up the budget spreadsheet" and they cannot, they have no access to the drive. You ask them to "check my calendar" and they cannot, no login.

The only things they can work with are whatever you physically hand them, paste in front of them, or read out loud.

That is Claude without MCP.

02
// the keychain arrives

Now you hand them a keychain.

On the keychain are several keys. Each one is shaped differently. Each one opens exactly one door:

Gmail key
Calendar key
Drive key
GitHub key
Slack key

one keychain, many keys

Suddenly your assistant can do real work without you fetching everything for them. You say "draft a follow-up to today's 2pm meeting" and they walk to the calendar room, look up the meeting, walk to the email room, draft the reply, and send it.

MCP is the keychain.
Each "MCP server" is one key on it.

03
// why every door needs its own key

"Why not just one master key?"

Because every app is locked differently. Gmail has its own language for "give me an email." GitHub has a completely different language for "give me an issue." Your database speaks SQL. There is no universal way to talk to all of them.

Claude → Gmail translator → Gmail

the "Gmail translator" is the Gmail MCP server

So the people who built MCP said: let us invent one common language (MCP). Then whoever owns an app builds a tiny translator that speaks both their app's language and MCP. Claude only learns one language.

Same trick as USB-C. Instead of every device needing its own charger, the world agreed on one plug shape. Now any USB-C charger works with any USB-C device.

MCP = USB-C for AI assistants

04
// a Tuesday at your desk

So what actually changes for you?

Same simple request, asked on two different Tuesdays. One Tuesday you do not have MCP connected. The next Tuesday, you do.

"Claude, summarize the last 5 emails from my boss."

// without MCP
Claude: "I would love to but I cannot read your email. Could you paste them?"

You open Gmail. You copy the first email. Paste. Copy the second. Paste. Ten minutes of copy-pasting later, Claude summarizes them.
// with MCP (Gmail on)
Claude reaches into Gmail through the MCP server, fetches the five emails, summarizes them.

One step. You did nothing extra.

That is the entire user-facing point of MCP. It turns Claude from "smart but locked out of your apps" into "smart and actually able to use them on your behalf."

05
// what YOU actually do

You flip switches. That is it.

If you are not a developer, you will never see JSON. You will never edit a config file. You will never run a server. You go into Claude's Settings, find Connectors, and toggle on the apps you want.

Settings  ›  Connectors
Gmail
Read and draft emails on your behalf
Google Calendar
Check your schedule, add events
Google Drive
Fetch and edit your files
Slack
Read messages, post replies
GitHub
Browse repos, read issues, open PRs

Behind the scenes, when you toggle one on, Claude starts up the matching MCP server. The "key" appears on the keychain. The next time you chat, Claude can use it.

The whole technical layer (JSON-RPC, config files, manifests, transports) is for developers wiring up custom apps that have no pre-built connector. For everyone else, MCP is the toggle switch and the result.
